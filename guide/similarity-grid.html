The Similarity Grid

In Act 3 you turn every chunk into a vector and search by how close the vectors are. Before you do, see what "close" means. Here are 8 short phrases from the campaigns, each compared with every other by meaning.

Done when

  • you've found a pair the model scores high with no word in common
  • you've found two phrases for one person that it scores low
  • npm run quest:similarity prints the grid

1 Read the grid

Each cell is the cosine similarity of two phrases, from openai/text-embedding-3-small. It measures how close the two vectors point: 1.00 is the same direction, and the lower the number, the less alike in meaning. The darker a cell, the closer the pair. A phrase against itself is always 1.00.

the toymaker and a toy shop in town score 0.61. They share no whole word, and the model still sees them as close. It matches meaning, whatever the words.

the toymaker and the blacksmith score 0.53, lower, but well above Blinsky and the toymaker at 0.16. Blinsky is the toymaker, yet the model scores the toymaker nearer a blacksmith, another trade, than nearer his own name. It doesn't know a name is a person, so a question that asks for him by name can miss the chunk about him.

The highest pair off the diagonal is Blinsky and Blinsky's Toys, at 0.69. Sharing a name pulls two phrases together, which is how the model can find his shop but not the man.

Pizza, pizza, pizza. scores low against everything. Table chatter lands far from the story, which is why a question about the story doesn't pull it in.

2 Run the side quest 

npm run quest:similarity

With a key set, it embeds the 8 phrases live, for under $0.000001, and works out the cosine similarity in plain TypeScript, the same math pgvector runs in Act 3. With no key, it prints the grid on this page and says so.

Now try your own. Put each phrase in quotes. It needs a key.

npm run quest:similarity -- "Blinsky" "the man who makes toys" "the man who sells toys"

3 What Act 3 does with it

In Act 3 each chunk gets one vector, and a question gets one too. Search returns the chunks whose vectors are nearest. A name, a place or a paraphrase can all land near or far from the question, and you'll read what comes back, mistakes included.
